Comparison:
  - OLTP  : 17308.074 ms   (sql/analysis/q3_readmission.sql)
  - STAR  :    12.405 ms
  - Improvement: 1395x faster
  - Parity: proven identical (8 rows, both EXCEPT directions,
            tests/12_parity_q3.sql), and the underlying flag proven row-by-row
            against the OLTP self-join across all 70,004 encounters
            (tests/14_readmission_flag.sql)

Why it is faster:
  The self-join was not optimised. It stopped existing.

  is_readmit_30d is a boolean column computed once during the ETL. The question
  "which specialty has the highest 30-day readmission rate" therefore becomes a
  GROUP BY over a boolean, served by the partial index idx_fact_inpatient, which
  covers only the ~12% of rows that are inpatient stays.

  The buffer numbers make the point better than the milliseconds:

      OLTP : 8,939,200 buffer hits   (~71 GB of logical reads, for 8 rows)
      STAR :     2,018 buffer hits
      ratio: 4,430x fewer pages touched

  The OLTP query re-scanned encounters once per index stay because there is no
  index on patient_id and the match is a date RANGE, which cannot be hashed. The
  star query reads a narrow slice of one table.

  This is the clearest example in the lab of what pre-aggregation actually buys.
  Q1, Q2 and Q4 got faster by 2-3x because their work was made cheaper. Q3 got
  1395x faster because its work was moved to load time and is not done at query
  time at all. Those are different kinds of win, and blurring them into an
  "average speedup" would misrepresent both.

The cost, stated honestly:
  is_readmit_30d is DERIVED data. Three consequences:

  1. If the clinical definition changes from 30 days to 45, this column must be
     RELOADED. No query edit can fix it. days_to_next_encounter is stored beside
     it precisely so the threshold stays auditable and re-derivable without
     returning to the source.
  2. It depends on the FUTURE. An inpatient stay discharged on Monday only
     becomes a readmission when the return visit arrives, so an incremental load
     must recompute already-loaded rows at least 30 days back, not just insert
     new ones.
  3. It can silently drift from the source definition. That is exactly why
     tests/14 compares the flag row-by-row rather than trusting the 8 aggregated
     rows in tests/12, where two opposite errors could cancel out. A fast,
     confident, wrong answer is the failure mode this whole design invites.

On the cohort translation:
  Q3's index stays are `encounter_type = 'Inpatient' AND discharge_date IS NOT
  NULL`. The star equivalent is `f.is_inpatient AND f.discharge_date_key <> -1`,
  because the ETL maps a NULL discharge to the dim_date Unknown member. That
  second predicate matches 0 rows today - every generated encounter has a
  discharge date - but without it this stops being a translation of Q3 the moment
  one open stay exists, and it would fail silently rather than loudly.
