Schema Analysis:
  - Tables joined   : encounters (twice - self-referencing), providers,
                      specialties
  - Number of joins : 2 explicit INNER joins, plus a CORRELATED SUBQUERY that
                      re-reads encounters once per index stay
  - Rows scanned    : 8,493 index stays, but the subquery re-scans encounters
                      for each one
  - Buffers touched : 8,939,200
  - Rows aggregated : 8,493 -> 8 output rows

Bottleneck Identified:
  This is the slowest query in the lab by two orders of magnitude, and the
  timing alone hides why. The buffer count is where the story is.

  8,939,200 buffer hits, at 8 kB per page, is roughly 71 GB of logical reads to
  produce 8 rows. Compare Q1's 653 buffers for the same underlying table. The
  cause is the correlated EXISTS: for each of the 8,493 inpatient index stays,
  Postgres re-scans encounters looking for a later visit by the same patient.
  8,493 x ~640 pages per scan is ~5.4M, and the plan reports 8.9M once the
  aggregate's overhead is included.

  The plan shows the split cleanly: the joins and the index-stay scan finish in
  well under a second; the GroupAggregate node then runs from 1,269ms to
  9,380ms. Roughly 8.1 seconds - about 92% of the query - is spent evaluating
  the SubPlan.

  Two structural reasons it cannot be fast in this schema:

  1. There is no index on encounters.patient_id. Foreign keys do not create one
     in Postgres, and 01_schema.sql deliberately does not add one, because that
     is the honest OLTP shape - the transactional system has no reason to carry
     an index that only analytics needs.

  2. Even with one, the match is a RANGE condition on dates
     (encounter_date > discharge_date AND <= discharge_date + 30 days). Ranges
     cannot be hashed, so the planner cannot turn this into a cheap hash
     semi-join the way it does for Q1's equality joins.

  The cost also scales quadratically. Measured across dataset sizes: 7,000
  encounters -> 83ms; 30,000 -> 1,412ms; 60,000 -> 5,556ms; 600,000 -> over
  265,000ms. Doubling the data roughly quadruples the time, which means this
  query does not merely run slowly today - it stops being viable at all as the
  hospital's history grows.

  Correctness notes (both deliberate):
  - EXISTS rather than a JOIN. A patient returning twice within 30 days would be
    counted twice by a naive join, producing rates above 100%. EXISTS flags each
    index stay at most once.
  - The specialty attributed is the INDEX stay's provider, not the returning
    visit's, because the index stay is the care being judged.
  - Strictly `>` discharge_date, so a same-day transfer is not a readmission.
